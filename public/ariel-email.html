The largest therapeutic-food factory in Africa deserves a data spine worthy of the children it feeds.

A letter to the leadership of Ariel Foods FZE — from the engineer who built a complete factory digital platform from inside a factory floor, in Epe, minutes from Alaro City.

Dear Sir/Madam,

A sack of peanuts, a bag of milk powder, a roll of foil sachets — none of them save a child by themselves. What turns them into hope is a system that proves every batch is safe, precise and traceable, unbroken all the way from a northern Nigerian farm to a sachet opened under a tent in Borno. My entire career has been quietly preparing me to build that system for Ariel Foods.

My name is Damilare Paul Ayodele, and I am writing to offer myself to Ariel Foods FZE as your Chief Software Architect. I am currently a Quality Control officer at Starium Rafa Detergents in Epe — where I have served since February 2026 — and I am also the person who, outside my official duties, single-handedly designed, built and deployed a complete enterprise resource planning platform for that factory: fully functional, running live in my own test environments today, and ready to serve operations the moment management approves deployment. I write to you from Iraye-Oke, Epe — minutes from your gates at Alaro City — and I will do the same work for you, on your terms, for the most important customer of all: a malnourished child.

01 The Problem I Could Not Ignore

Every batch of RUTF and RUSF that leaves Alaro City must satisfy the most demanding customers in the world — UNICEF, WFP, USAID and the governments that buy therapeutic food for malnourished children. Those agencies do not accept memory, notebooks or "trust us" in an audit room. They ask: which lot, which supplier, which test results, which moisture and aflatoxin levels, who signed the release — and they ask it instantly and provably.

I did not learn this truth from a slideshow. I learned it standing at factory machines holding a clipboard. At Starium Rafa Detergents I watched the exact gap that exists in food plants across this country: critical numbers — densities, weights, waste, machine stoppages, pallet movements — written by hand into paper notebooks, transcribed hours later, aggregated days later, acted upon sometimes never. Managers were deciding from memory; supervisors walked the floor to discover facts that were already an hour old; every network outage froze what little digitisation existed. In a detergent plant, that gap costs money. In a therapeutic-food plant, it costs children's lives — because a batch that cannot be proven safe cannot be released, and a batch stranded between notebooks cannot feed anyone.

I concluded that the distance between what happens on a factory floor and what leadership knows is not a people problem. It is a software problem. And nobody was coming to solve it. So, on my own time, at my own expense, I began to solve it myself.

02 What I Built — While Doing My Day Job

The Starium Rafa Enterprise ERP is complete and running live in my test environments today: twenty-plus routes covering the operational spine of a manufacturing company, real-time on every device, resilient against power and network failure, and costing nothing in licences or servers. It is finished, proven end-to-end, and ready to enter daily factory service as soon as management approves its deployment.

QC Command Suite

Inspection & Batch Lineage

String weight, bag & carton inspections with cooldown logic, batch lineage and dual sign-off — a full genealogy trail per production unit.

Waste & Yield Intelligence

Machine & Silo Health

Pallet & Stores Ledger

Floor-to-warehouse movement with auto-computed quantities, teams and waybill-ready records — shelf-stable inventory, audit-ready.

Underneath it sits an offline-first architecture: every entry is preserved locally the instant it is made, then synchronises automatically when connectivity returns. It is hosted redundantly across three independent providers because in manufacturing, availability is not a feature — it is the product. In humanitarian manufacturing, it is the difference between a batch released on time and a batch stranded until an engine fails.

03 Imagine

Imagine every peanut lot from a Kaduna or Kano smallholder farm logged the moment it passes your gate — weight, moisture, mycotoxin screening, supplier, farm co-op — and following that lot, invisible and unbroken, through roasting, milling, blending with milk powder and the 23 vitamins and minerals, to the exact foil-sachet lot number an auditor asks about next year.

Imagine your roasters, blenders and fillers talking to you by the second — temperatures in validated ranges, mixing ratios drifting nowhere — alerting the operator before a deviation becomes a disposal, exactly the way my machine-health and silo modules already watch over a detergent plant today.

Imagine quality results captured on tablets at the lab bench, batch release approved with named digital signatures, and a compliance pack — HACCP-ready, UNICEF-ready, NAFDAC-ready — generated in minutes instead of days, for every audit, every tender, every donor report.

None of this requires waiting for the future. Every piece of it is technology I have already shipped once — built inside a real factory's walls, under real constraints, proven end-to-end in my own live test environments, on my own initiative. It is what I intend to bring to Alaro City.

04 Why Ariel Foods. Why Now.

Ariel Foods is not merely a manufacturer; it is the largest ready-to-use therapeutic food facility in Africa — built at Alaro City in under a year, shelf-stable for 24 months at 30°C, producing the peanut-based supplements that treat severe acute malnutrition in the most fragile settings on the continent. Your founder, Mr Dhiren Chandaria, has said the goal plainly: Africa serving Africa, manufacturing locally what Nigeria once imported by the tonne.

And you are standing at the precise moment where software stops being a convenience and becomes strategy. The US$2.5 million UNCDF investment under the Nutrition Supplier Finance Facility — the UNCDF–UNICEF partnership with the Children's Investment Fund Foundation — is putting new equipment on your floor specifically to process more locally grown peanuts, soy and chickpeas, bringing as many as 1,500 Nigerian smallholder farmers into your supply chain and helping partners reach an estimated 25,000 more children every year by 2030. That mandate is, at its core, a data mandate. Imported peanuts arrive with certificates; local peanuts arrive with stories. Turning those stories into audit-grade, traceability-ready supply-chain data is exactly the engineering I do. And your joining the UN Global Compact in May 2026 means Ariel will publicly report its progress against the Sustainable Development Goals — a system can make that reporting effortless instead of heroic.

There is also a simpler truth: I live in Epe. Alaro City is minutes from my home. I already serve a nutrition-focused business network through my consultancy work, and I have spent the last eight months living the daily rhythm of a factory floor. When a silo alarm fires or a QC result fails at 2 a.m., your software architect is ten minutes away — not ten time zones away. To me, the success of this plant is the transformation of my own community's greatest asset, led from within.
