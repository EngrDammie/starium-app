01

The four core pricing models

There is no single "best" model — only the right one for a given scope, client and outcome. Most successful freelancers run two or three at once: retainers for stable income, fixed projects for upside, hourly only for genuinely uncertain work.

₹

Hourly

Sell time · scope is uncertain

You bill actual hours worked. The client carries the risk of the work taking longer.

✓ Safe for unknown scope, debugging, audits, new clients ✗ Caps your income; punishes you for getting faster
¶

Fixed / Project

Sell a deliverable · scope defined

One price for a clearly defined deliverable. You keep the margin when you deliver fast.

✓ Rewards speed; client knows the total cost upfront ✗ Scope creep eats margin without a change-order clause
⟳

Retainer

Sell availability · recurring need

A fixed monthly fee for a defined scope or hour bank. Steady, predictable income.

✓ Kills feast-or-famine; paid in advance ✗ Can become underpaid employment without scope + SLA
★

Value-Based

Sell the outcome · measurable value

Price at 10–20% of the measured value your work creates — revenue gained, cost saved.

✓ Highest ceiling; income decoupled from hours ✗ Needs a value both sides agree on in writing before you start

Rule of thumb

Year-1 freelancers usually run 80–100% hourly. By year five, the best-run books are 0–20% hourly, split across retainers (stability), fixed projects (upside) and one productized or value-based offer (scale). Pure hourly is the lowest-earning model after your first 18 months.

02

The decision rule — pick the model before you quote

Ask three questions in order. Whichever question stops you cleanly tells you the answer.

1

Is the scope clear & stable?

If the client cannot describe "done", you cannot price done. Unclear scope → hourly or retainer. Fixed-pricing a fuzzy scope is the single most common way freelancers lose money.

2

Is the work recurring?

Ongoing needs (hosting, updates, features, support) → retainer, paid in advance. Accept a modestly lower effective rate in exchange for predictability — predictability has real monetary value.

3

Is the outcome measurable & attributable?

Value-based pricing needs all three: measurable in money, cleanly caused by your work, and agreed in writing before you start. If a client disputes measurement later, the model collapses.

Default when nothing decides it

Fixed price on a tightly defined deliverable. It caps the client's uncertainty, and it makes getting better directly profitable. Then protect it with a written change-order clause.

Your floor rate — the number you never go below

An hourly rate that only covers delivery time is not a rate — it's a subsidy you're paying your clients. Your floor covers your real life, blank months, taxes and business costs. Everything below it is you losing money.

With Nigeria's inflation consistently above 20%, a rate you set two years ago is a 40–50% pay cut today. Review every 6 months and raise 15–25% a year. Give existing clients 30–60 days notice. Raise immediately when you're booked 3+ weeks out or you've shipped a strong new proof.

04

Naira clients vs Dollar clients — two currencies, two books

This is the single biggest leverage decision in the playbook. International clients always get quoted in their own hard currency; local clients in naira. Mixing them quietly bleeds you.

The naira trap

Quoting an international client in naira anchors them to a number that shrinks in dollar terms every time the naira weakens. Dollar clients → quote in USD. Always. Local clients → naira, on fixed terms.

Maintenance, hosting & subscriptions — turn builds into rent

A one-off build pays you once. The same client on a monthly care plan pays you 3–4× over a year. This is the healthiest revenue line in the whole business — build it into deals from day one, not as an afterthought.

Three ways to sell ongoing care

♦

Hour-bank retainer

A set pool of hours per month (5 / 10 / 20) with a response-time SLA. Unused hours roll over up to a cap (e.g. 50%), then expire.

Small apps often start around $300–$800/mo.

◈

Managed care plan

Hosting + updates + backups + security + monitoring bundled into one monthly fee. Sell reliability, not line items — never let the client compare your price to a $5 shared host.

Solo sweet spot ≈ $100 – $200/mo per site.

◉

Continuous-development retainer

Maintenance plus a monthly feature backlog, QA and regular releases. The client buys an outcome and access, not tracked time.

Frequently-evolving products: $500 – $2,000+/mo.

Hosting math that compounds

One $1,500 build with no care plan = $1,500 total, ever. The same client on a $300/month care plan = $5,100 over the year — 3.4× the revenue, and it doesn't reset to zero in January. Even five clients at $99/month is ₦800,000+/year of rent on work you'd be doing anyway. Bundle hosting into the plan; define scope in writing; cap content edits.

Value-based pricing — price the outcome, not the hours

Your proof (a factory ERP that replaces paper, cut reporting effort, stop guessing from memory) is a value story. When a client can name the money your work saves or makes, you charge a share of it — not a multiple of your time.

08

Presenting & closing — the anatomy of a quote

How you present the number decides whether it closes. Lead with the deliverable and its value, state your terms in the same breath as the price, and let the client choose between scope options — never between you and a lower rate.

1

Anchor to the deliverable

"$8,500 for a fully responsive 8-page site, SEO setup, CMS and 2 rounds of revisions, in 4 weeks." The client evaluates a package — not whether you're worth $170/hr.

2

Terms in the same breath

Payment schedule is part of the price. Under $15K → 50% deposit, 50% on delivery. Larger → 40% start / 30% midpoint / 30% delivery.

3

Pushback → adjust scope, never rate

"I can bring it to $6,000 by cutting to 5 pages and 1 revision round." Dropping your rate teaches clients your prices are negotiable every single time.

4

Never lead with hourly

Show project prices and retainers only. A public hourly rate anchors every negotiation on time instead of outcome — and caps what you'll earn.

Reframe script when they ask your hourly rate

"I structure engagements around outcomes rather than hours, because it aligns our incentives — you know exactly what the final cost is, and I'm motivated to be efficient. For this project the investment is [₦X], which covers everything needed to hit [the outcome]."

Scope protection — the clauses that save your margin

Every fixed price needs armor before you start. These are the standard protections that separate a professional quote from a leaky one.

Risk multiplier — how to load uncertainty into a fixed price

multiply, don't guess
FactorRangeWhen it applies
Scope ambiguity1.0 – 1.5×Vague client; thin brief; requirements still moving
Technical complexity1.0 – 1.4×Real-time features, hard domain logic, scaling
Integration risk1.0 – 1.3×Payments, third-party APIs, external systems
Client maturity1.0 – 1.3×New relationship; approval chains; slow responses
Timeline pressure1.0 – 1.2×Rush delivery, weekend/evening work (add 20–30%)
A clean project ≈ 1.1× total. An ambiguous, rushed, integration-heavy job can justify 2.0×+. A well-defined spec should only need the +15% buffer; a vague one, +50–65%.
  • Change-order clause: anything beyond the written spec is billed at a stated rate, pre-approved in writing. 1–2 revision rounds max included.
  • Explicit exclusions: list what's NOT included (copywriting, integrations, 3rd-party licences, migrations) so "can you just add…" is answered by the contract.
  • Milestone billing, not completion billing: deposit + milestones keep you never more than one phase from money. Terms discussed after the work is done are terms you've lost.
  • Scope-creep threshold: e.g. deviations beyond 15% of the approved spec trigger the change-order process automatically.
  • Payment term in the proposal: net-7 or net-14 for balance, penalties for late payment, access/credentials released only after final payment.
Fixed-price discipline

Estimate hours honestly (worst case), multiply by your target flush rate, then add the buffer — and after every delivery, calculate your effective hourly rate. If two of your last three projects pay below your target, your estimating is the problem, not the market.

10

Golden rules — the dos and don'ts of getting paid properly

A dozen lessons compressed from the market data and practitioner experience above.

✓

Charge a deposit

Upfront payment is both cash flow and a filter — it removes clients who were never going to pay well. 40–50% is standard.

✓

Price in phases

Bill discovery separately and quote a range until requirements are locked. Undefined scope bid low is how freelance horror stories start.

✓

Maintenance from day one

Quote the build with the care plan beside it. Converting old clients later is fine — selling it into the original deal is better.

✓

Rush premium

48-hour turnaround → +25–50%. Weekend/evening work → +20–30%. Difficult-client tax → +15–25%. Ask for it; it works.

✗

Don't default to hourly

Hourly is for genuinely uncertain scope only. For repeatable deliverables, project pricing earns ~27% more on average in effective terms.

✗

Don't discount to win

Undercutting teaches the client your work is negotiable. Adjust scope to fit budgets; never the rate.

✗

Don't quote stale naira

With 20%+ inflation, a rate set two years ago is a silent pay cut. Review every 6 months; raise 15–25% yearly.

✗

Don't make retainers infinite

Defined scope, capped hours/edits, monthly report, quarterly renegotiation. Otherwise the retainer becomes an unpaid salary.
