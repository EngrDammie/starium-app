02 Why the paper form breaks

The form works. It has worked for years. But it has four specific problems, and one of them is a genuine arithmetic error sitting in the photograph HR sent.

Problem 1 — The numbers do not add up

Here is what the filled-in form actually says:

BoxValue written by hand
Annual Leave Days entitled21
No. of days already taken8
Total Days this Vacation4
Outstanding No. of Days after this Vacation11

But 21 − 8 − 4 = 9, not 11. So one of three things is true:

🥇 Most likely: carry-over

The employee carried 2 days over from 2025. Then 23 − 8 − 4 = 11. This matches perfectly.

🥈 Possible: a public holiday

A holiday inside the range made some days non-working. But 5–8 October has no Nigerian public holiday, so this is less likely here.

🥉 Also possible: a slip of the pen

Nobody is claiming HR is wrong. But this is exactly the class of mistake a human makes at 4pm with a queue at the door.

💡
This is actually the best argument for the whole project

A machine does not get tired and does not mis-add. Once the computer works out the numbers, this class of mistake disappears completely — and we will never have to ask HR which of the three explanations was correct.

We will build carry-over into the system anyway, because it is the most likely explanation and it is standard practice. If HR later says the answer was something else, it is one setting.

Problem 2 — Everyone retypes the same details

Look at the first six boxes: Name, Cadre/Designation, Department, SAP No., Shift. These never change between one leave form and the next. Yet HR and the employee write them out again, every single time, by hand, on every single form. That is five boxes of pure copying — and copying is exactly where mistakes get made.

Problem 3 — Nobody can see who is away

Approved leave lives in a folder. If the HOD wants to know whether three people from the same machine crew are off at once, the honest answer is "let me go and check the folder". In a factory, that answer causes a stopped line nobody knew was short-staffed.

Problem 4 — Balances live in someone's head

"How many days has Ahmed got left?" The answer is a sum, spread across a folder, done by memory. Nobody is lying — the number is just not written down anywhere a computer can read.

🎯
The one-line summary

The paper form asks humans to be calculators and typists. Both are things computers are dramatically better at. Our job is to keep the humans doing the parts that need judgement — approving, and covering the work.

03 What we are building

Two new modules inside the app that already runs the factory. Nothing is replaced; everything that works today keeps working.

The short version

✅
In one sentence

A list of every employee, and a leave system that fills in the paper form for you, works out the days for you, and sends it round for three signatures.

The two modules

👥 Module A — Employee Roster

One record per person: SAP number, name, job title, department, shift, phone, email, date joined. HR fills it in once. Every leave form ever after pulls the details from it automatically.

Bonus: this page is already advertised in the app's sidebar menu but has never been built. It is a dead link users can click today. We are closing it.

📅 Module B — Leave Management

The digital version of the paper form. Staff fill it in on their phone. The app works out the days and the balance. Supervisor, HOD and Head of HR approve in order. HR gets a printable form that looks like the paper one, plus reports.

Also included: "who is on leave today" on the Command Center dashboard, and a year-end balance report.

Why employee records must come first

This is the most important structural decision in the project, so it is worth explaining slowly.

Right now, the app stores a list of people who can log in. That list holds a first name, a last name, an email address and some permissions. It has no SAP number. No job title. No department. No shift. It is a door-key list, not an employee record.

Leave Management needs all of those missing pieces. So we build a proper employee record first.

⚠️
The mistake we are deliberately avoiding

A tempting shortcut is to bolt the new fields onto the existing user list. Do not do that.

It would break the moment someone needs leave but does not need a login — and in a factory, that is very common. A cleaner hands over their work at the end of a shift. A driver signs a delivery note. They never open the app. If they are not in the user list, they are not in the system at all.

So: every employee gets a record. A login is optional and simply links to it. A person can have a record with no login, and that is normal, not an error.

04 The 24 assumptions we are shipping

HR has not answered any questions yet — deliberately, because the point is to show him something working first. So we are shipping sensible, professional defaults. Every single one can be changed later without touching code.

🔧
This is the whole trick

Leave policy is not written in the code. It lives in the app's settings screen, where HR can edit it. Changing the annual entitlement from 21 days to 24 is a typing job in System Config, not a job for a developer.

That is why we can build this now and correct it later without rewriting anything.

05 What Nigerian law already requires

We are not inventing policy. These are the legal floors the system must never drop below. Whatever HR decides, these are the minimums.

LeaveThe legal minimumSourceWhat we assume
Annual leave At least 6 working days with full pay, after 12 months' continuous service. (12 days for staff under 16.) An employer may agree to defer it, but never beyond a 24-month earning period. Labour Act, s.18 21 days — well above the legal floor, as the paper form shows
Maternity leave 12 weeks — six weeks before and six after. Not less than 50% of wages where the employee has served 6 months. Labour Act, ss.54–58 12 weeks, fully paid
Sick leave Up to 12 working days per calendar year, where certified by a medical practitioner. Labour Act, s.16 Not on the current form. Can be added as a sixth type
Rest day One day of rest in every seven, of at least 24 consecutive hours. Labour Act, s.14 Sundays are non-working (A7)
Payment in lieu Unlawful to pay wages instead of the holiday while the contract continues. Payable only on leaving, pro-rata. Labour Act, s.18(3)–(4) Out of scope (A22)

Why this matters commercially

Starium is above the statutory floor on annual leave already. Keeping an accurate record of entitlement, taken days and approval is how a company stays demonstrably compliant. When a staff member disputes a balance two years later, the answer is a query, not an argument.

This is a quiet benefit of the module that never appears on a feature list, and it is a real one.

🚫
Where we disagree with the standard advice, on purpose

Every product in the research suggests auto-approving short leave. We are not doing it.

The reasoning is factory-specific: in a plant, one absent operator can stop a line. The cost of a person missing is not the same as the cost of a meeting being missed in an office. A human should see every single request. If HR disagrees later, it is one setting (A19).

Two ideas from the research worth stealing

🔢 "Overlap" and "conflict" are different

Mature systems distinguish two things. An overlap means a colleague is also away — just information. A conflict means the department's coverage limit is reached — that deserves real attention.

We do the same, with a threshold of two (A15). It stops the app crying wolf on every request while still catching the real risk.

📒 Every credit needs a reason

Best-in-class systems write an entry every time a balance changes, so a number is never "just a number". If someone asks why a balance is 9, there is an answer.

Our equivalent: the balance is derived from the request history rather than stored in a counter. The history is the ledger, so there is nothing that can drift out of step. Explained in section 08.

07 Decision 1 — People first, then leave

Employee Onboarding is not something we are adding "because it seemed useful". It is a hard dependency. Skip it and Leave Management cannot work.

The dependency, plainly

The leave form asks for five identity fields: Name, Cadre/Designation, Department, SAP No., Shift. The app currently stores none of them.

🔗
So there are only two honest options

Option A: HR retypes every employee's details onto every leave form. The app then does nothing useful — it is a prettier clipboard.

Option B: The app holds the employee details once, and every leave form fills itself in.

We are building Option B. That single decision is what turns this from a form into a system.

What each employee record holds

FieldWhy we store it
SAP numberThe staff number the company already uses. Must be unique — it is how HR thinks about people
First and last nameAppears on the leave form and every approval
Cadre / DesignationJob title, straight from the paper form
DepartmentDrives the approval routing and the coverage warnings
ShiftDAY or NIGHT — used when checking who is away from a given shift
Phone, email, addressPre-fills the "contact while on leave" boxes
Date joinedNeeded to work out pro-rated leave for new joiners (A10)
Employment statusActive, inactive or suspended. Inactive people keep their history — we never destroy it
Linked login (optional)Set only if this person has an app login. Left empty for staff who never log in

Why the login is optional, and what "linked" means

When someone logs in, the app matches their email against the employee list and links the two. If no match exists, they simply have no employee record — and they see a clear message asking them to contact HR. Not an error, not a crash, not a blank balance of zero.

🔐
One thing we must not break while doing this

The app has a heartbeat that records who is online, and a comment in the code explaining that the "goodbye" signal must be sent before the logout clears the session — reversing those two lines causes a permissions error and leaves a ghost user online.

We are adding a whole new identity lookup into the same file. That comment is load-bearing and stays exactly where it is. It is written in the build task precisely so a future developer does not tidy it away.

"But how does someone request leave if they have no login?"

This is the fairest objection to the whole design, so it deserves a proper answer. There are two routes, and both are built.

📱 Route 1 — They get a login

A login and permission to see factory data are two different things. A cleaner does not need to see quality records, production data or reports. They need exactly one thing: their own leave screen.

So the Employee Roster screen gets a "Create login" button. One click, and that person has an account that can see only My Leave — a single page, no factory module of any kind.

Giving the whole plant a login is therefore no longer a project. It is a list HR works down.

🧑‍💼 Route 2 — HR lodges it for them

An operator tells HR they are away next week. HR types it into the app on their behalf, in about fifteen seconds. The form arrives at the supervisor exactly as if it came from the employee.

Mature leave products treat this as a normal admin capability, not an emergency workaround.

🔑
Why Route 2 works beautifully — the payoff for one early decision

Every leave request is filed against the employee record's own ID, never against a login ID. Identity lives on the person; the login is only a convenience.

So when HR lodges a request for Robert Eneh, it is still Robert's request. It draws on Robert's balance. It appears in Robert's history. It is counted when HR asks who is away.

Nothing is orphaned, nothing is anonymous, and HR never has to keep a separate paper note "this one was spoken, not written".

🏷️
Both routes leave a clear trace

A request lodged by HR carries a "Lodged by HR" badge everywhere it appears, so an approver is never misled into thinking the employee filled it in themselves.

HR can also amend or cancel a lodged request — with a recorded reason. Both are normal admin actions, not workarounds.

⚠️
A trap in the existing code that this feature walks straight into

In the app today, a menu entry with no allowed roles listed means "super admin only", not "everybody". That is why System Config and User Roles are hidden from normal staff.

My Leave must be visible to everyone, including a person holding no department role at all. So it cannot simply reuse that empty list — and it certainly cannot list all eight departments, because that quietly breaks the day HR adds a ninth.

We add an explicit "open to everyone" flag instead, checked before the existing rule. Small change, and it stops a silent failure of exactly the kind this app has been bitten by before.

A small but real find

🔎
There is already a broken link in the app

The sidebar menu already offers "Employee Roster" and "Payroll" under Human Resources. Neither has a page behind it. An HR user can click them today and get nothing.

We are building Employee Roster properly. We are removing the Payroll link rather than shipping a menu that lies — a menu that promises a page and delivers nothing is worse than an incomplete one.

08 Decision 2 — The computer does the arithmetic

This is where the biggest quality gain is. Four boxes on the paper form stop being questions and become answers.

The four boxes that change

On paperOn screenWhat changes
Annual Leave Days entitled — staff write itRead-only, from the employee recordNever mistyped
No. of days already taken — staff adds it upRead-only, summed from approved requestsAlways current, including leave approved while this one is pending
Total Days this vacation — staff counts the boxesRead-only, updates live as dates changeSundays and public holidays handled correctly
Outstanding after this vacation — staff subtractsRead-only, updates liveThe 21 − 8 − 4 ≠ 11 class of error disappears entirely

The rule the system follows

🧮
Plain English

Count only the days someone would actually be at work. Skip Sundays. Skip public holidays. If leave starts on a Wednesday and ends the following Monday, that is 4 days — not 6.

Then work out what is left: entitlement + carry-over − already taken − this request. If the answer is below zero, say so and stop.

Why the numbers are copied onto the request, not looked up later

Suppose HR changes an employee's entitlement from 21 days to 24 next quarter. There is a leave request from February sitting in a folder, already approved, already printed and filed.

If the app looked the balance up live, that old request would suddenly display different numbers. The printed form in the cabinet and the app on the screen would disagree. That is how you lose trust in a system permanently.

📸
The rule

When a request is created, the numbers are photographed onto it — entitled, already taken, requested, outstanding. They are frozen for that request forever.

Approved records never change their arithmetic. Ever.

And why we do not store a balance that gets adjusted

The lazy way to build this is a running total: every time leave is approved, subtract from a number stored on the employee record. It looks efficient. It is a trap.

That number will eventually disagree with the list of leave requests. Then somebody has to write a repair script, and nobody can say which figure is right. It always happens — usually at year end, when payroll needs the figure and nobody can produce it.

📚
What we do instead

We add up the approved requests every time we need a balance.

The request history is the ledger. There is nothing to reconcile, so there is nothing that can go wrong. The number is always explainable, because you can click through and see exactly which requests made it up.

Three traps we are guarding against from day one

🌍 The timezone trap

JavaScript reads '2026-10-05' as midnight in London. In Lagos that is already the 5th at 1am — but the same string read the other way round becomes the 4th. Every single day count would be off by one and nobody would notice for weeks.

🗓️ Leave year ≠ calendar year

If Starium's leave year ever starts in July, a request dated March 2026 belongs to leave year 2025, not 2026. Getting this wrong resets everyone's balance in the wrong month.

➖ Backwards dates

If someone picks an end date before the start date, a naive subtraction returns −4 days. The result is stored, and every future balance for that person is corrupted.

✅
All three get an automated test

The app already has a pattern for testing date logic in isolation, with no database and no network. We follow it exactly, and all three of these traps become tests that fail loudly if anyone breaks them later.

09 Decision 3 — Three signatures, in order

The paper form's Section 3 has three signature blocks side by side. Copying that arrangement into an app would be a mistake. Here is why.

The app already has an approval system. It is the wrong shape.

The factory app already signs off shift reports. Two people approve them, and either can sign in any order. That is correct there — a shift report is a snapshot of a team. Everyone signs the same thing, and nobody's signature depends on anyone else's.

Leave is different. The Head of HR signing means the supervisor signed first, and the HOD confirmed the handover was settled. It is a chain of authority, not a group signature.

❌ How the shift report works

Supervisor signs or QC Manager signs. Whoever gets there first. Both end up signed.

Correct for a shared snapshot. Wrong for leave.

✅ How leave must work

Supervisor signs → HOD is now allowed to act → HOD signs → Head of HR is now allowed to act → Head of HR signs → leave is unlocked.

Each stage is locked until the one before it is done. Always.

How it looks to the employee

Not a technical table — a progress strip. The employee can always see exactly where their request is sitting, and who holds it now.

👀
The test that matters most

Find the one way this can go wrong: a supervisor who is also the HOD. If that person can approve both stages in one sitting, the chain is broken.

There is an explicit test in the build plan for exactly this case. It is the single most likely thing to get wrong in the whole module.

The permissions behind the buttons

Three new approval powers are added to the app's existing role system, so a manager sees only the buttons that belong to them:

  • Leave Supervisor — can sign at stage one only
  • HOD / Subsidiary — can sign at stage two only
  • Head of HR — can sign at stage three, the final gate

These are assigned in the existing User Roles screen. No new screen is needed, and no one accidentally gets a power they should not have.

🧾
Every decision is recorded, permanently

Each stage stores who signed, what their role was, when, and any note they left. Nothing is ever overwritten.

This is the audit trail for a decision that affects someone's pay. If it is ever questioned, there is a complete record rather than a conversation about what was agreed.

10 Decision 4 — Works offline, with one deliberate exception

The whole app is built offline-first, because factory Wi-Fi fails. We follow that rule everywhere except one place, and we want to explain exactly why.

The rule the app already follows

📡
Offline-first, everywhere

Every existing module — carton waste, laminate, pallet transfer, QC checks — tries to save to the database. If there is no signal, it saves to a waiting list on the device and sends it automatically the moment the connection returns. Staff on the factory floor keep working.

Leave submissions will behave exactly the same way. A staff member with no signal can still lodge a request.

The one place we break the rule

🛑
Approvals will NOT work offline

A request may be written offline. An approval may not.

Consider what "approve" means. It authorises someone's paid leave. If that decision sits in one browser's memory until Wi-Fi returns, then for a while the official record of who approved paid leave does not exist. On a document with legal weight, that is not acceptable.

So when an approver opens the app with no signal, the buttons are disabled and the screen says so plainly: "Approval needs a connection. Nothing has been saved — reconnect and try again."

💡
Why we are writing this down

Every other module in this app queues offline. A future developer will see approval buttons sitting next to offline-capable code and assume it is a bug. We write it into the code comments and into this document so nobody "fixes" it by adding offline queuing to approvals.

Exceptions are dangerous in code unless they are written down where the next person will actually find them.

One more thing that must not break

The app registers each module in a single list, and that list drives both the "waiting to send" counter and the automatic retry. Adding leave means adding one entry to that list — not editing the sync engine in five places.

There is also a stability warning written into the app about those counters: if the functions that report them change identity on every render, the page enters an endless update loop and crashes. Our addition must not disturb that. It is called out again in the build plan and the regression task.

The printable form is not optional

🖨️
This is the screen that decides whether HR says yes

HR keeps the physical file. If our system cannot produce that exact artefact, we have not saved anyone anything — we have moved the problem back to a clipboard.

So the printable form reproduces Section 1, Section 2 and Section 3 exactly as they appear on the paper, populated with real names and real dates on the signature lines, with a space left for a wet signature.

The screen managers will actually open

HR's real question is not "show me the leave records". It is "who is not on the floor right now?"

So the Command Center gets a card showing how many people are away today, who they are, which department, and when they are back. That is the single highest-value screen in the module, and it is a small piece of work.

🔒
Who sees what

The dashboard card shows names and dates only — not reasons. A compassionate leave reason is deeply personal and can involve family bereavement or illness. It stays with the employee and the approvers who need it.

Approving someone is different from being entitled to know why they are ill. The system draws that line deliberately.

Why this order

1

People before paperwork

Nothing in Leave Management can be built before the app knows who the employees are. Phase 1 has no alternative position.

2

Settings before anything reads them

Putting the policy in configuration means the engine is built against values HR can change, rather than values baked into the code.

3

Day counting is the part that must be provably correct. Build and test it with no user interface in the way, then build the form on top of something already proven.

14 What could go wrong

Five things that would genuinely damage this work, and what we are doing about each. Stating them up front is more useful than discovering them later.

Risk 1 — A new collection is added without a matching rule

🔴
This has already happened once in this app

Several record types were added without a matching permission rule. Writes were refused, the app quietly saved them to a waiting list instead, and everything looked like it was working while nothing reached the database.

What we do: an automated test checks that every record type has a matching rule, and the new types are added to that test the same day they are created — not at the end of the project.

Risk 2 — Someone's leave is visible to the wrong people

⚠️
A hidden menu item is not a locked door

The app hides menu entries a user should not see. But anyone can type a page address directly. Every new screen needs its own check inside the page, not just a hidden menu item.

What we do: a phase-7 task tests the whole permission matrix role by role, including the case where a supervisor tries to approve out of turn.

Risk 3 — Breaking what already works

⚠️
This is the risk I take most seriously

Leave touches shared settings: the menu, the routes, the configuration, the offline registry and the permission rules. Those five files are load-bearing for the 21 modules already running. A careless edit to any of them can break live production data capture.

What we do: a dedicated regression task runs the full test, lint and build suite and then exercises the existing modules by hand — pallet transfer especially, because it exercises the offline counters we are touching.

Risk 4 — A day count is off by one

⚠️
The failure that damages trust fastest

An off-by-one error does not crash anything. It just quietly gives everyone the wrong balance, and nobody notices until year end.

What we do: all the date logic is written as plain functions with no database attached, then tested against real scenarios — a full week, a request across a Sunday, a request across a public holiday, and the year turning over. The timezone trap gets its own test.

Risk 5 — HR approves the demo and then changes the policy

🟢
This one is designed out

This is the whole reason for section 04. Because every policy decision lives in configuration rather than in code, a change of mind from HR is a settings edit — not a rebuild, not a delay, not a billable change.

See section 15 for exactly how long each change takes.

The one change that is bigger than it looks

⚖️
If entitlement varies by grade

Assumption A3 is a flat 21 days for everyone. If HR says it depends on seniority, we add a per-grade table: grade, then days.

The engine already reads entitlement from a single place, so this is a small addition rather than a rewrite — but it is the one assumption most likely to turn out this way, so it is worth raising early rather than discovering in March.

What happens to requests already approved

Nothing. This is decision 2 from section 08, and it matters for a practical reason.

Approved requests keep the exact numbers they were approved with. If HR changes the entitlement tomorrow, historical requests do not rewrite themselves, and the printed forms in the cabinet still match what the app shows.

📁
What that means in a handover

Changing a policy is safe to do at any time. Existing balances will update, because balances are recalculated from live request data. Existing approved requests will not, and are not supposed to.

That is the correct behaviour, and it is worth saying out loud to HR so it does not look like a bug.

16 How to demo it

Five minutes, on a phone if possible, because that is how staff will actually use it.

Sample data to seed first

Twelve or so fictional employees across the departments, realistic SAP numbers, and leave requests in every possible state — waiting at each approval stage, approved, rejected and cancelled. Use obviously invented names.

🌟
Include one request that spans a public holiday

That single case is the most convincing thing in the whole demo. Choose a request that crosses a declared holiday and show that the app correctly refuses to count the holiday as a leave day — while the paper form would have needed a human to notice.

It demonstrates the day-counting engine doing real work in about two seconds.

The running order

  1. Open the roster. Every employee, with details filled in once. "This is the part HR retypes onto every single leave form."
  2. Log in as a staff member. Open My Leave. The balance is already there — name, title, department, SAP number. Nothing to type.
  3. Pick dates. Watch the day count and the remaining balance update as you drag. Try a range across a Sunday and a holiday.
  4. Submit. Name who is covering the work. Hand it over.
  5. Log in as the supervisor. Approve. Log in as the HOD — the button was greyed out until a second ago, now it works. Approve. Then Head of HR.
  6. Print the form. This is the moment. Watch their face.
  7. Open the Command Center. "Who is on leave today", live.
  8. Export the report. Year-end balances in a spreadsheet.
🎬
Record it

Screen-record steps 3 to 6. It lets HR show management without you in the room, and it is a genuine demo asset for the live system.

17 Words explained

No jargon left undefined. If a word appears in this document, it is explained here.

Pro-rated
Giving someone a share of the full allowance, based on how long they have worked. Someone who joined halfway through the year gets roughly half the annual entitlement.
Entitlement
How many days someone is owed for the year. A policy number, not something anyone decides per request.
Working day
A day the person would actually be at work. Sundays and public holidays do not count. A leave request is measured in working days, not calendar days.
Carry-over
Leave left unused at the end of a year, kept for next year. Usually capped — at Starium's assumed 5 days — so a large unused balance cannot build up and block future requests.
Leave year
The 12-month period the allowance is measured against. Assumed to be the calendar year, but changeable.
Balance
Entitlement plus carry-over, minus everything already taken. The number an employee is allowed to request against.
Overlap
A colleague is also away at the same time. Informational only — often perfectly fine.
Conflict
Enough people from one department are away that coverage is genuinely at risk. Warned about, and worth a conversation.
Handover note
A short record of what work someone is passing to a colleague before they leave. Without it, a line can stop during an absence and nobody knows why.
HOD
Head of Department. The second signature on the leave form.
SAP number
The staff identification number the company already uses. It identifies a person across payroll, leave and any other system.
Offline queue
A waiting list saved on the device. If the network is down, records are held there and sent automatically when the connection returns.
Audit trail
The permanent record of who decided what, and when. It cannot be edited by the person it concerns.
Resumption date
The day someone comes back to work — the next working day after their leave ends, calculated automatically.
Snapshot
Copying a value onto a record so it is frozen there. We snapshot leave balances so an approved request's numbers never change afterwards.
Sequence
One step after another. Leave approvals are sequential: each signature unlocks the next.
